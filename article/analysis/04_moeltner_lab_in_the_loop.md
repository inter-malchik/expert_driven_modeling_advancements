Gindullina et al.'s work (hereafter **Paper A**) introduces Expert-Guided PINN: an epidemiologist comments on a SIRD-PINN forecast, a hierarchical classifier maps the comment to modification rules, and an LLM rewrites the composite loss function before retraining. Tobias Möltner et al.'s work, *"Creation, Evaluation and Self-Validation of Simulation Models with Large Language Models"* (hereafter **Paper B**), addresses the adjacent problem in multibody dynamics: LLMs generate `Exudyn` simulation code from textual model descriptions, then a second LLM agent validates trajectories through *in silico* numerical experiments in a **lab-in-the-loop** framework (Fig. 5) rather than trusting compile checks alone.

The comparative link is direct. Paper A's Future work names an "LLM-expert-evaluator" and multi-agent autonomy but leaves forecast adequacy to a human viewing a plot; Paper B implements that evaluator role at scale with 18 selectable physics-check methods and reports when conjecture-based validation fails but trajectory-based validation succeeds. Both groups benchmark overlapping models (Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.3-70B) on physics-oriented Python codegen and document that syntax success does not imply physical or semantic correctness.

Below: where the pipelines align, where Paper B supplies a tested blueprint for Paper A's documented gaps, and five transplantable extensions.

---

## 1. Expert-in-the-loop versus lab-in-the-loop

Paper A keeps the epidemiologist in the acceptance loop:

> **Quote (Gindullina et al.):** *«A key contribution of this study is an interactive framework that instantiates an expert-in-the-loop optimization cycle. The process begins with an expert's qualitative assessment of a forecast, which is translated into a structured specification for loss function modification... The loop is closed by presenting the refined forecast to the expert.»*

Paper B removes humans from routine validation and nests two agent loops—code generation outer, per-model validation inner—using methods chosen from a catalog of 18 evaluations (Table 4 in Paper B):

> **Quote (Möltner et al.):** *«Within the model validation framework, performed as in-silico experiments with AI agents, there are two nested loops. The outer loop runs over all available models... while the inner loop performs a validation for a single model.»*

> **Quote (Möltner et al.):** *«Fig. 5: Schematic overview of the lab-in-the-loop framework.»*

Paper A validates loss code with a Python interpreter; Paper B validates *simulation outcomes* against physics tasks. The architectural gap is not domain (epidemiology vs. mechanics) but *where* truth is checked: syntax tree versus trajectories.

---

## 2. Paper B as blueprint for Paper A's multi-agent Future work

Paper A explicitly proposes evaluator agents it does not build:

> **Quote (Gindullina et al.):** *«A promising direction is to evolve the system toward a multi-agent architecture, where specialized LLMs enhance autonomy and objectivity. An LLM-expert-evaluator could quantitatively assess the correspondence between the forecast and the original comment; low confidence scores could trigger automatic loss regeneration or comment refinement. An LLM-loss-semantic-checker could verify alignment between the generated code and modification rules, reducing hallucinations.»*

Paper B is a working instance of the first agent: an LLM scores simulation output on a 0–100 scale from model description, evaluation method, sensor placement, and numerical results—without exposing the generating code to the evaluator. Paper A's semantic checker remains named only; Paper B's lab-in-the-loop stack is the closest evaluated analogue in the series.

---

## 3. Conjecture-based validation fails; trajectory inspection succeeds

Paper B tested two validation philosophies and abandoned the first:

> **Quote (Möltner et al.):** *«Conjecture-based evaluation: The first method is based on using the conjectures... generated by the LLM as the reference... However, this method produced largely erroneous results, and therefore has been abandoned. The root cause lies in the disconnect between the generation of the conjecture and the actual simulation model.»*

> **Quote (Möltner et al.):** *«Direct evaluation: The second method bypasses the conjecture entirely and instead evaluates the raw numerical simulation results using the selected evaluation method. Here, another LLM call is used to interpret the numerical data and determine whether the outcome is physically plausible and consistent with the task. This method yielded significantly more reliable results than the conjecture-based method.»*

Paper A's PINN Customizer effectively asks the *same* LLM to both invent loss weights and judge epidemiological plausibility implicitly—there is no separate trajectory audit before the expert sees the plot. The 700-day outbreak case (weakened boundary condition, plausible curve) is exactly the class of error conjecture-in-head cannot catch but post-training $S,I,R,D$ series inspection could.

---

## 4. Rigid modification rules versus a library of physics checks

Paper A constrains codegen with four ontology classes and Table A.2 hard limits:

> **Quote (Gindullina et al.):** *«These rules, provided in Appendix A, represent strict guidelines for LLM, defining which components of the original loss function can be modified (e.g., by adding a penalty term) and which are prohibited.»*

Paper B offers 18 flexible evaluation methods (momentum conservation, damping, symmetry, planar motion, etc.) from which the LLM selects eight per model. The design philosophies differ: Paper A *prevents* edits; Paper B *probes* behavior after generation. For epidemiology, analogues would be population conservation $S+I+R+D=N$, monotonic cumulative mortality, and wave decay—automating intermediate gates Paper A currently delegates to the physician.

---

## 5. Shared LLM benchmarking: 8B fragility, 70B syntax, semantic gaps

Both papers stress-test overlapping open models on physics-related Python.

Paper A on Llama-3.1-8B:

> **Quote (Gindullina et al.):** *«The Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct model showed poor results, as half of the generated loss functions were uncompilable, and 20% of all runs ended in errors.»*

Paper B on the same family:

> **Quote (Möltner et al.):** *«Using symbols such as L (e.g., for the length parameter) led to a significant drop in performance, since the symbol was used but no value was assigned, especially in Phi-4 and Llama-3.1-8B.»*

Paper B reports Llama-3.3-70B at 78.9% physics correctness in simulation; Paper A reports 100% first-try compilability for 70B but only 25–27% "Compliance with the comment." Cross-paper lesson: compile-ready code is necessary but insufficient—a pattern Paper B quantifies with TPR/FPR on validator agents.

---

## 6. Prompt perturbation: hidden instability in NL→code pipelines

Paper B documents sensitivity invisible to single-shot tests:

> **Quote (Möltner et al.):** *«Our findings reveal that even superficial changes to the textual description of mechanical models can induce significant differences in LLM generated code, both in terms of structure and correctness.»*

Paper A normalizes expert comments through a Conversational Agent but does not run systematic paraphrase/whitespace perturbations on comments before PINN Customizer invocation. Given Paper A's temperature=1.0 prototyping setting and 20-run evaluation, unmeasured prompt variance may contribute to compliance variance alongside model scale.

---

## 7. In-context templates versus free-form loss editing

Paper B raises compilation stability via rigid object templates in prompts (Listing 1 pattern; QwenCoder-32B-Q4 at 99.4% executability in Paper B's Table 2). Paper A's LLM writes free-form PyTorch penalty math—a mode Paper A's own Future work plans to replace with class-subclass templates:

> **Quote (Gindullina et al.):** *«We plan to replace the simple penalty additions by class-subclass-specific modification templates. These templates will explicitly define which loss components and parameters can be altered, which mathematical operators are admissible, and what numerical ranges are permitted.»*

Paper B demonstrates that template-constrained codegen plus post-hoc lab validation beats raw generation; Paper A proposes templates but measures only the raw path today.

## Proposed direction 1. Epidemiological lab-in-the-loop after PINN retrain

**Pipeline stage:** Retraining loop → pre-expert gate (new validation stage).

**Mechanism:** After each loss edit and PINN retrain, run a fixed battery of checks on $S,I,R,D$ trajectories (population balance, monotone $D$, decay of $I$); an LLM or rule engine scores plausibility on resampled series—mirroring Paper B's direct evaluation without exposing loss source code.

**Failure mode:** >700-day outbreak from weakened $I(T_{max})\to 0$ boundary.

---

## Proposed direction 2. Class-subclass API templates instead of free-form loss code

**Pipeline stage:** PINN Customizer.

**Mechanism:** Replace raw PyTorch generation with typed calls (`AddPeakShiftPenalty`, `AddPostPeakDecayPenalty`) as in Paper B's `Exudyn` ICL listings; LLM extracts parameters from comments only.

**Failure mode:** Literal numeric insertion without semantic adaptation.

---

## Proposed direction 3. Perturbation benchmark on expert comments

**Pipeline stage:** Evaluation protocol.

**Mechanism:** Replicate Paper B §2.6: paraphrase/whitespace variants of each comment; measure TSED dispersion and compliance F1/FPR across hundreds of runs (Table 5 analogue).

**Failure mode:** Unstable compliance under innocent wording changes.

---

## Proposed direction 4. Specialized local coder model for PINN Customizer

**Pipeline stage:** PINN Customizer (model selection).

**Mechanism:** Deploy QwenCoder-32B-Q4 or similar on-institute hardware (Paper B: 91.7% correctness, 99.4% executability) instead of API-only general models—reducing latency and data egress for sensitive incidence series.

**Failure mode:** Llama-3.1-8B 50% non-compilable loss functions.

---

## Proposed direction 5. Reframe LLM-expert-evaluator on trajectories, not code

**Pipeline stage:** Evaluation (implements Future work evaluator).

**Mechanism:** Score alignment between comment and forecast using validator LLM on trajectory features only—per Paper B's rule that evaluation input excludes $\text{code}_{k,i,j}$—triggering regeneration when score $<$ threshold before human review.

**Failure mode:** 15–28% of edits yielding no meaningful forecast change despite "success" on secondary criteria.

---

## Conclusion

Moeltner et al. show that grounding LLMs in physics means closing the loop through simulation, not syntax alone: conjecture-based self-judgment fails, trajectory-based lab-in-the-loop validation partially succeeds, and prompt perturbations demand rigorous F1/FPR reporting. Gindullina et al. already sit on the same codegen frontier with overlapping model benchmarks and name the missing evaluator agents—but stop at compile checks and expert plots. The strongest reading is not "replace epidemiologists with automation," but transplant Paper B's *validation architecture* into Paper A's pipeline: templates at generation time, trajectory batteries at verification time, and the LLM-expert-evaluator Paper A already sketched in Future work.
