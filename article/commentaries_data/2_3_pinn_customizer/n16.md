Paper A (Gindullina et al., «Expert-guided forecasting of epidemic ARI incidence based on physics-informed neural networks and large language models») evaluates success of loss modifications against four qualitative criteria with MAE thresholds of 700 and 1100, chosen empirically, and against subjective compliance with the comment at n=20 and temperature=1.0. Each iteration includes expensive PINN retraining, and the authors do not separate retraining noise from the effect of the LLM edit.

## Sources & Evidence

This section maps the card’s claims to verbatim citations (English) from the local transcription: “2016 Landscape Features for Computationally Expensive Evaluation Functions: Revisiting the Problem of Noise.md”.

1) Claim: "Expensive evaluations demand cheap diagnostics; however, cheap features can be extremely sensitive to even small noise."
   - Evidence: Scott & De Jong (2016), Abstract; §1 Introduction (lines 20–28).
   - Quotes (verbatim):
     > "there is a need for landscape features that are both (A) possible to estimate with a very limited budget of fitness evaluations, and (B) accurate in the presence of small to moderate amounts of noise. We show via a small set of relatively inexpensive landscape features ... that these two goals are in tension with each other: cheap features are sometimes extremely sensitive to even very small amounts of noise."

     > "These applications tend to involve fitness functions that are very expensive to compute—each evaluation taking on the order of seconds, minutes, or even hours to complete."

2) Claim: "Noise sensitivity is measurable; some standard hill‑climb features become inaccurate already at tiny noise levels (≈1e−3)."
   - Evidence: §3.1 Sensitivity Analysis (lines ~97–101; Fig. 2).
   - Quotes (verbatim):
     > "in general we find that features 1–4 are extremely sensitive to noise: the estimate becomes inaccurate as soon as p reaches a value of about 10^{-3}."

3) Claim: "Averaging multiple samples (explicit averaging) is not an effective fix."
   - Evidence: §3.2 Explicit Averaging (lines ~111–114), Eq. (2) (line 83).
   - Quotes (verbatim):
     > "We find that using explicit averaging of fitness has very little discernible impact on the accuracy of fitness measurements. Even at N = 15, a great deal of error remains."

     > Eq. (2): \(\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N F(\boldsymbol{x}).\)

4) Claim: "Population‑based methods can reduce noise effects for feature estimation (with trade‑offs)."
   - Evidence: §3.3 Population‑Based Search (lines ~117–119); Conclusion (lines 131–135).
   - Quotes (verbatim):
     > "Replacing the hill-climbers in these features with a population-based algorithm does overcome noise, but it changes the kind of information that the features gather from the landscape."

5) Claim: "Noise model and tiny‑p regime explicitly considered in the study."
   - Evidence: §2.1 Test Functions — Eq. (1) (lines 65–69).
